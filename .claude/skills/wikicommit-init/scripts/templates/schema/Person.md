  provenance: default
  granularity:
    - Create a new page for any person mentioned by full name as an independent subject
    - Use WikiLink [[Person/slug]] in body text for incidental mentions
    - "A person a work of fiction is *about* is an independent subject, not an incidental mention. A narrative states independent facts about its protagonist — what they do, their circumstances, how they are placed in the world — which is exactly the bar the rules below apply; that the person is fictional does not change this (schema:Person covers fictional as well as real people by definition). Create a page for the person a story, novel, or biography is about, and for a named character who recurs across two or more works this wiki covers. Do not create one for the rest of the cast, who act only inside one work's plot without that work being about them — this rule adds at most one page per work plus recurring characters, and is not a licence to promote every named character (Issue #560)"
    - A person named only as a co-author, citation source, or someone else's collaborator within another entity's source — with no independent facts about that person (their own background, views, actions) stated in the source — is an incidental mention, not an independent subject, even though their full name appears; do not create a page for them
    - A single quoted remark or opinion attributed to a person, with no other biographical or contextual information about them in the source, does not by itself meet the "independent facts" bar above — treat it the same as an incidental mention and do not create a page. Independent-subject treatment requires the source to say something about who the person is or does (background, role, actions), not merely to quote a single sentence from them
    - Do not record marital/family relationship properties (spouse, children) or physical attributes (height, weight) even when the source states them — out of scope for this wiki's purpose
    - "For a living individual, keep the page to what that person has themselves made public — works they wrote, talks they gave, positions they say they hold — and leave out biography-shaped facts that would need primary-source verification, such as career history, past employers, and dates of birth, even when the source states them. This is the same judgment the rule above makes about family and physical attributes; the failure it guards against is a person page carrying dates and claims taken from an article that was never itself taken in as a source (Issue #473). It narrows what a page says, never whether the page exists — that is a separate question, answered by .wikicommit/entity-policy.md and off by default. It applies only to the living, so write a historical figure in full (Issue #668). A work of fiction's portrayal of a living individual is not something they made public; which page holds it, and when the person's page may carry it, is the next rule's living-person branch (Issue #1341)"
    - "A real person a work of fiction refers to — the work names that historical or living person and has them act in its story — keeps one page, the real person's, and the work's portrayal is a section of it rather than a page of its own. While the work is the page's only source, the page may hold only the portrayal, but its first sentence frames it as the work's (\"In <work>, X appears as …\") and nothing on the page states the portrayal as the person's history; a section on the documented life is added when a source about it comes in. A person the author made up is a separate subject even when modelled on a real one — a character, never a section of the real person's page; when the rule above gives the character a page, its first sentence names the work and says the character is fictional. Mention the real model only when the source states the relation. For a living individual the living-individual rule above takes precedence while the work is the only source, even when the work is about them: write the portrayal only on the work's own page (where it tells its characters) and do not create the real person's page — a page built from someone else's fiction about a living person would be all third-party claims, and a reader could take the portrayal for the person's own record. Create the page when a source about the person comes in, write it from that source under the living-individual rule, and only then add the portrayal as a section framed as the work's (\"In <work>, X appears as …\"); framed that way it states what the work does, not a fact about the person, so the living-individual rule does not exclude it (Issue #1341)"
    - "For a character, write who they are in the work — their part in it, their traits and situation, how they stand to the other characters — and do not retell the plot event by event. The plot belongs to the work's own page; a character page that walks through each scene they appear in repeats it there. For the same reason, leave out what the work keeps back until late — how it ends for them, or a twist such as their being the culprit — and let the work's plot section be the one place that tells it; a premise the work states at the start is not kept back. Name the characters they come up against even when the meeting comes at the end; leave out only how it turns out"
    - Boundary — a Person is an individual human. A role, job title, team, or shared account standing for a group is not a person — use Organization for a group that acts as a single actor, and DefinedTerm for a role or title the wiki treats as a concept in its own right

```text
READER QUESTIONS (Person)
Readers: someone who met the name in another page or a source and wants to know who this is
and why the wiki has a page on them — a real person, or a character in a work.
1. Who is this? For a real person, what they are known for and, unless they are living,
   when and where they lived; for a character, which work they appear in, that they are
   fictional, and their part in it.
2. What did they do that matters to this wiki's subject — the works, positions, decisions,
   or views the source attributes to them? For a living individual, only what they have
   made public themselves.
3. For a real person who is no longer living only: how did their life or career unfold, as
   far as the source tells it? (A living individual is excluded — see granularity.)
4. For a character only: what kind of person does the work make them — their traits,
   situation, and how they stand to the other characters — rather than what happens to
   them scene by scene?
5. For a character only: which works or episodes do they appear in, and what is their part
   in each (a table when there are several), without how each work ends?
6. For a real person a work of fiction portrays only: how does the work portray them? Keep
   this apart from what the page says of their documented life. For a living individual,
   not while the work is the only source: the portrayal then stays on the work's page and
   the person gets no page yet (see granularity).
7. How have others received or judged them — reputation, influence, criticism — as the
   source reports it?
```
